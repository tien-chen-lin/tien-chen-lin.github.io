**How does a neural-cell interface control shape?** Neurons and glia exchange biochemical signals, contact-dependent cues, and force while their cytoskeletons continuously reorganize. My lab asks which of these inputs move the neuronal cytoskeleton between exploratory, polarized, stable-growth, and repair-permissive states — and whether those transitions can be predicted and reset.

I work across the full causal chain: from cytoskeletal biochemistry and reconstitution, through acute optogenetic control and quantitative live imaging, to sparse perturbation in developing tissue and dynamical modelling. This approach revealed that **neuronal polarity emerges from an intrinsic Arp2/3–actomyosin oscillator** (*Nature*, 2026). The independent program now moves from the neuron alone to its physiological interface with glia — using microtubule nucleation, ageing, and focal cortical dysplasia as bounded mechanisms or applications rather than separate lab identities.

### The program, in three parts

- **Generate and predict neuronal cytoskeletal states.** Define the variables that distinguish oscillatory exploration, persistent growth, retraction, and stable polarization; use acute perturbation and held-out predictions to test the model.
- **Identify the interface input.** Separate soluble, contact-dependent, substrate, and mechanical contributions from glia, and determine the causal input–output law.
- **Stabilize, fail, and reset.** Test how actin–microtubule organization stabilizes a selected state, and how one ageing, reactive, or disease context alters the measured control variable.
