## Research

<div class="section-card" markdown="1">
### What we found

Neuronal polarity is not dictated part by part — it is *selected*. Working in cultured neurons and in developing tissue, we showed that the soma, not the growth cone alone, acts as the central organizer: an intrinsic **ARP2/3–actomyosin oscillator** running across the whole cell breaks symmetry, and whole-cell competition between actin networks selects the axon (*[Nature](https://rdcu.be/fsRqy)*, 2026).

That result reframes neuronal morphogenesis as a **dynamical-systems problem**: a cell's shape is the output of a self-organizing system whose regime is set by definable control parameters, not a fixed blueprint. It also raises the two questions my laboratory is built around.
</div>

<div class="section-card" markdown="1">
### Two open problems

**1. What controls the oscillator?**
A neuron in tissue does not oscillate in isolation. Its dominant physiological neighbor is glia, which can act through several distinct channels at once — and those channels are usually conflated. The open question is *which class of glial input actually moves the neuronal system between states*, and by what input–output law. Mechanics is the leading candidate, not an assumed answer.

**2. How does a transient decision become permanent?**
Selecting a state takes minutes; holding it takes a lifetime. **Consolidation** — how transient cytoskeletal decisions are consolidated into persistent neural architecture — is the part nobody has explained. I ask how actin–microtubule organization locks in a chosen state, and what makes that lock fail.

Both are approached the same way: define the state variables that distinguish oscillatory exploration, persistent growth, retraction, and stable polarization; perturb an input acutely; and test the model against held-out predictions rather than post-hoc fits.
</div>

<div class="section-card" markdown="1">
### Where the framework is tested

Bounded applications of the mechanism — contexts in which the control variable can be measured and moved, not separate laboratory identities.

- **Aging and repair.** Whether the control variable shifts in an aged or reactive context, and in which direction, is an open measurement rather than a foregone conclusion.
- **Cortical malformation.** Focal cortical dysplasia as a setting where cytoskeletal state control is disrupted.
- **Microtubule nucleation.** Non-centrosomal nucleation as a mechanism feeding the same control problem — connecting the γ-tubulin (γ-TuSC / MOZART1) nucleation biochemistry of my training to the living neuron.
</div>

<div class="section-card" markdown="1">
### Capability chain

Identify a physiological input, measure the state of the force-generating system, perturb that input acutely, and test whether the resulting transition can be predicted and reversed. That requires an unusual span:

- **Optogenetic & chemogenetic control of cytoskeletal proteins** — light-induced dimerization and targeted degradation for spatiotemporal control in defined cell types.
- **Primary neuron–glia co-cultures** — to observe how an altered glial cytoskeleton reshapes neighboring neurons.
- **Sparse conditional perturbation *in vivo*** — AAV / *in utero* electroporation with cortical slice culture, manipulating cytoskeletal genes in single cells while preserving the surrounding tissue.
- **Protein biochemistry & *in-vitro* reconstitution** — linking molecular activity to the mechanics and geometry of cytoskeletal networks.
- **Advanced imaging** — live-cell, super-resolution, TIRF, and tissue clearing, with electron-microscopy collaborations for ultrastructure.
- **Quantitative state inference and modeling** — extracting state variables from live imaging and testing them predictively.
</div>

<div class="section-card" markdown="1">
### Earlier work

- **The biochemistry of microtubule nucleation.** I established MOZART1 as an integral part of the γ-tubulin small complex and a driver of its assembly into a nucleation-competent ring, and dissected the mitotic phospho-regulation of γ-TuSC and its receptors — reconstitution of an active microtubule-nucleation template (*J. Cell Biol.* 2016), and how phosphorylation licenses nucleation in space and time (*eLife* 2014).
