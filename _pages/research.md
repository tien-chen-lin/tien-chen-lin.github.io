How does a transient growth advantage become a durable identity—and when can that identity change?</p></header>

<section class="editorial-section" id="foundation">
<div class="section-heading" markdown="0"><p class="eyebrow">The foundation · Nature 2026</p><h2>An oscillator<br>helps select an axon.</h2></div>
<div class="section-body" markdown="1">
My first-author study identified an intrinsic cytoskeletal oscillation originating at the soma. ARP2/3- and myosin-II-dependent activities coordinate repeated neurite extension and retraction before persistent axonal growth emerges.

I led the experimental development and quantitative analysis, combining acute perturbations, live imaging, local optogenetic manipulation, and specialist collaborations. With Andreas Husch, I re-established local single-cell perfusion for spatial perturbation.

The study explains how recurrent dynamics contribute to **initial axon selection**. It opens a distinct next problem: what preserves the selected neurite’s axonal identity through remodeling, and how does that capacity change as a neuron matures?

<section class="editorial-section" id="questions" markdown="0">
<div class="section-heading" markdown="0"><p class="eyebrow">The independent questions</p><h2>Preserving one identity.<br>Restricting another.</h2><p>The launch focus is the interval after initial axon selection, while compartment identity remains developmentally plastic.</p></div>
<div class="section-body">
<div class="question-row"><span class="question-number">01</span><div><h3>When does the selected axon become resistant to reassignment?</h3><p>Track individual neurons across maturation, apply a transient cytoskeletal perturbation, and follow recovery after washout. Does the original axon retain or re-establish its identity, does another neurite become the axon, or has stable identity yet to re-emerge?</p><p>A further question asks why a previously selected axon preferentially regrows after retraction. Candidate explanations include retained structures, an adhesion-associated path, or an advantage that existed before selection.</p></div></div>
<div class="question-row"><span class="question-number">02</span><div><h3>When can a competing neurite acquire—and retain—axonal identity?</h3><p>Use local, reversible perturbation to promote one competing neurite at defined developmental stages. Follow it after withdrawal while tracking the original axon. This separates temporary extension, transient axonal features, a persistent additional axon, and replacement of the original axon.</p></div></div>
<p class="tissue-note"><strong>A testable hypothesis.</strong> Maturation progressively limits axonal reassignment and persistence of an additional axon. These restrictions may arise at different times and depend on different processes.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="editorial-section" id="approach">
<div class="section-heading" markdown="0"><p class="eyebrow">The experimental strategy</p><h2>Perturb locally.<br>Withdraw.<br>Follow the same neurite.</h2></div>
<div class="section-body" markdown="1">
**Growth and identity are different readouts.** Longitudinal imaging will be combined with live polarity or transport markers, independent axonal and dendritic staining, and later axon-initial-segment formation. Developmental stage, growth history, recovery, and neuronal health will be measured alongside fate.

**From cellular difference to molecular mechanism.** Actin dynamics, microtubule growth and movement, array organization, polarized transport, and adhesion provide candidate explanations. The process best supported by measurements will guide causal perturbations. Molecular analysis and biochemical reconstruction will then focus on mechanisms implicated in living neurons.

**Models that guide experiments.** Quantitative analysis will test whether cytoskeletal measurements predict later growth and identity beyond cell shape and developmental history. Competing explanations will be compared through perturbations that distinguish their predictions.

**A matched tissue comparison.** Cortical projection neurons from matched embryonic cohorts will be followed in dissociated cultures and organotypic slices. An early compatibility track will establish interpretable imaging and perturbation in tissue; subsequent comparisons will ask whether tissue changes acquisition, persistence, or the relevant cytoskeletal process. Soluble signals, contact, extracellular matrix, and mechanics become mechanistic questions when an observed difference points to them.

<div class="research-sequence" markdown="0"><span><b>01</b> Define developmental history</span><span><b>02</b> Apply &amp; withdraw a perturbation</span><span><b>03</b> Follow recovery &amp; later identity</span><span><b>04</b> Test the causal mechanism</span></div>
</div>
</section>

<section class="editorial-section" id="horizon">
<div class="section-heading" markdown="0"><p class="eyebrow">The longer-term direction</p><h2>Selective change<br>within a stable cell.</h2></div>
<div class="section-body" markdown="1">
The broader question is how one cellular region can acquire new properties while neighboring regions preserve their organization. Axon-carrying dendrites offer a natural example of adjacent compartments with different identities and developmental histories.

Once a developmental stabilization mechanism is established, it can motivate tests of whether the same mechanism constrains conversion of a dendrite into a replacement axon after axotomy. These comparisons will distinguish shared developmental mechanisms from injury-specific processes.

The long-term goal is to explain how cytoskeletal dynamics and developmental history let specialized structures persist while selected regions remodel.
</div>
</section>

<section class="editorial-section" id="biochemistry">
<div class="section-heading" markdown="0"><p class="eyebrow">A complementary foundation</p><h2>Reconstructing<br>cytoskeletal machinery.</h2></div>
<div class="section-body" markdown="1">
My earlier research defined phosphorylation-dependent regulation of γ-tubulin-complex assembly and established how MOZART1 and complex receptors cooperate to form an active microtubule-nucleation template. I independently initiated the MOZART1 project, obtained an EMBO Short-Term Fellowship, and established recombinant purification and reconstitution.

This training provides a route from a neuronal phenotype to a defined molecular interaction: engineer a perturbation, measure activity directly, reconstruct the mechanism where appropriate, and return the prediction to the cell.
